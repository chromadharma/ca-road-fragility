# The Road Is the Evacuation Plan: What California Could Actually Fix

*Sahasrik Ragani*

At ten in the morning on 8 November 2018, the Camp Fire was burning along Pentz Road, Bille Road and Pearson Road in Paradise, and the people on those roads were not fleeing it. They were sitting in it. The National Institute of Standards and Technology, reconstructing the fire minute by minute, describes civilians "stuck in traffic and trapped in their vehicles". The fire had started three and a half hours earlier near Pulga, some twelve kilometres away. Its first spot fires had landed in Paradise by 07:44, and its main front reached Pentz Road around 08:30. I wanted to know whether the people on Pentz Road ever had a chance. The answer, as far as a road network can give one, is that they did not, and that the reasons they did not are still sitting in the road network of most of the hills around them.

I built a model of the roads out of Paradise and its neighbours, Magalia and Concow, the way they were on the morning of the fire: every drivable road in OpenStreetMap, each one given a capacity in vehicles per hour, and every one of the 32,198 vehicles that census data says the ridge held before the fire. Then I asked how long it takes to get all of them to a freeway that is not itself in a fire zone. If the evacuation had been run perfectly, with every car sent down exactly the right road at exactly the right time, the ridge takes between two and a half and three and a half hours to empty (the range is honest uncertainty; most of these roads have no lane count recorded, so I tested both one and two lanes each way). Ordered at the moment of ignition, a perfect evacuation would still have been on the road when the fire arrived. Left to themselves, with every driver taking whatever looks fastest, the same 32,000 cars take over nine hours. The fire did not need nine hours.

This is the part of the story that is usually told as a tragedy of too few roads, and it is only half right. Paradise did not have one way out; it had six independent routes, and the model found the roads they run on without being told: Skyway, Clark Road, Pentz, Neal, Honey Run, Highway 32 and the road down the Feather River canyon. The problem is that six two-lane roads through the forest cannot carry a town of that size in the time a wind-driven fire allows. California already has a law about this. Senate Bill 99 (2019) asks every city and county to identify residential areas in hazard zones without at least two evacuation routes. Paradise would have passed. A rule that counts roads without asking what they can carry, or what they run through, would have certified the town as safe.

So I built a ladder instead of a pass mark. The bottom rung is SB 99's own standard: two routes. Above it sit the questions SB 99 does not ask. Can the community empty in two hours, the time it took the Camp Fire's main front to reach Paradise? In one hour, the time its first spot fires took? And can it do that using only roads that do not themselves pass through land CAL FIRE rates as Very High hazard? Across Butte County and the two Los Angeles fire areas of January 2025, around the Palisades and Altadena, fewer than three residents in ten live somewhere that can get out within an hour without driving through Very High hazard (29% in Butte, 23% in the LA clusters). In Butte, fourteen of thirty communities, home to 18,293 people, have no way out that avoids it at all.

Why keep a rung that is easier than the strictest one? Because a standard nobody can meet is not a standard; it is a press release. The ladder lets a county see where each community stands and what it would take to move it up one step, while the top rung stays on the page as a record of who is still exposed. I do not think the easier rungs are safe. I think they are the ones that can be funded.

The more useful discovery is that the same label, "no safe way out", covers three very different problems, and they do not have the same price. The Eaton Fire's evacuation group (Altadena, Sierra Madre and Kinneloa Mesa, about 40,000 vehicles) had the roads. A managed evacuation clears it in under an hour. Left to self-routing, the model sends almost everyone toward the same freeway entrance and down a handful of local streets (Eaton Canyon Drive among them), and the last car is out after more than thirteen hours. That is a problem of coordination, and coordination is cheap: phased evacuation orders by zone, officers at the right intersections, signals timed for outbound traffic. The Palisades is the opposite case. Its managed evacuation is capped by one road, Palisades Drive, which carries the Palisades Highlands down at around 950 vehicles an hour and runs through what became the burn area. No amount of traffic management widens a single road. Paradise is both at once, which is why it failed so badly.

The numbers on what each fix buys are the part I would put in front of a planning committee. Coordinating traffic saved between six and thirteen hours across the three fires; a hundred and fifty to a hundred and seventy-five lane-kilometres of new road saved just over one. On the Paradise ridge, managing the evacuation instead of leaving it to drivers cuts nearly six hours. Five rounds of widening on the three roads that bind (Clark once, Pentz once, Neal three times over: a hundred and seventy-four lane-kilometres of construction on some of the steepest terrain in the county) cut about another hour and a quarter, and still leave the ridge short of the two-hour mark. In the Palisades, management saves over ten hours, and a hundred and fifty-seven lane-kilometres of widening (Palisades Drive, the Pacific Coast Highway and three smaller roads) saves under an hour and a half. Reversing inbound lanes on the roads that bind (contraflow, which costs cones and people rather than concrete) cuts about half an hour on its own. The one place where construction is plainly the answer is the one where a single road binds: the Palisades Highlands need a second way down, and so do Berry Creek and Cohasset in Butte, the two communities that fail even SB 99's two-route test.

I also tested the fix everyone reaches for first, which is to open the fire roads: the unpaved tracks and gated service roads that thread through the hills and are left out of any normal road map. They help more than I expected with SB 99 and less than I hoped with anything else. Berry Creek and Cohasset each go from one route to three once the tracks are counted, which would carry both over the legal line. But not one of the fourteen cut-off communities in Butte gains a way out that avoids Very High hazard, because the fire roads run through the same forest as the paved ones. A second route through the fire is still a route through the fire.

Where none of this works, the honest recommendation stops being about roads. A building code does not let a theatre sell more tickets than its exits can empty in a fire; the number of seats is set by the number of doors. Thomas Cova asked two decades ago whether fire-prone communities should have a maximum occupancy on the same logic, and on the Paradise ridge the model says the doors could not empty the room in time at its 2018 population, whatever was spent on widening them. That leaves the options planners are least comfortable with: evacuation orders triggered by weather forecasts rather than by smoke, designated refuge areas for the people who cannot get out, and limits on how many more homes a ridge with six exits should take. My thoughts on the last of these are still developing; it is easy to write "occupancy limit" and much harder to say whose home it applies to.

There are limits to all of this, and they belong on the page with the findings. The model assumes every vehicle leaves and that demand is fixed; it does not simulate a fire moving through a town hour by hour, and it cannot say what fuel treatment along a road would buy. Lane counts are missing for most rural roads, which is why the Paradise figure is a range. As far as I could find, the state has set no target clearance time (its evacuation planning guidance was still a draft in 2025), so the one-hour and two-hour rungs come from the Camp Fire's own timeline rather than from any regulation.

California will spend billions on wildfire in the next decade, most of it on the fire itself: suppression, fuel breaks, hardened homes. The roads out get a line in the general plan. What the network says is that the cheapest hours are the ones gained by running an evacuation instead of letting one happen, that the places which genuinely need new road are few and nameable, and that for some ridges the question was never how to get everyone out faster but how many people should be up there in the first place. The people on Pentz Road at ten that morning were not failed by a lack of roads. They were failed by a plan that assumed the roads would be enough.
